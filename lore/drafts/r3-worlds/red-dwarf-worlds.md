Our adopted local stellar model assigns red dwarfs a fraction of 0.75.
That is a nominal approximation, not a measured fraction at that precision.
For these stars we adopt 0.16 planets/star in temperate rocky orbits from Kepler's
occurrence estimates. Neither number establishes a living world.

Temperate orbits around a cool, dim star lie close in. Planets there circle
quickly and may keep the same face towards their sun. A permanent day need
not make the whole world unlivable: an atmosphere or ocean could carry heat
into the dark. Whether either survives is another question.

Young red dwarfs can remain brighter than their settled state for a long
time. Radiation and activity can strip an atmosphere or help a planet lose
water before its present orbit becomes temperate. Water loss can also leave
oxygen without biology. Oxygen alone would therefore be an ambiguous clue.

A quiet red star today does not tell us the whole history of the world
beside it. Kepler's counts give us reason to look; they do not settle its
climate or biology. We include these worlds in our search and mark their
habitability as debated. An observational constraint takes precedence over
that possibility.
